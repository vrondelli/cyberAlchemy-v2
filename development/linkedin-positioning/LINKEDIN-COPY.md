## CyberAlchemy company page

### Tagline

Governed human–AI workflows for complex knowledge work

### About

CyberAlchemy is building a more accountable way for people and AI systems to work together on complex knowledge problems.

Our current work, CyberAlchemy v2 and DomainSpec v2, starts from a practical question: how do we move from intent to implementation without losing the reasoning, evidence, constraints, and ownership that make the work trustworthy?

We turn scattered research, decisions, and agent outputs into specifications, experiments, and artifacts that can be inspected, challenged, and continued. AI helps us explore, check, and propose. People remain responsible for judgment and direction.

The work is grounded in cybernetics, domain modeling, and formal methods. We use category theory and Lean to test specific ideas about what changes—or gets lost—when specifications are translated and refined.

CyberAlchemy is developing through focused projects, collaborative research, and implementation. The aim is not more automation for its own sake, but systems that extend human capability without making the work less understandable or accountable.

### Experience description

I founded CyberAlchemy and continue to build it with collaborators around a question that has become increasingly important to me: as more complex work moves through AI systems, how do we keep it understandable, challengeable, and accountable?

I lead the research, system design, and governance behind CyberAlchemy, with current work centered on CyberAlchemy v2 and DomainSpec v2. We design governed human–AI workflows and typed domain specifications that preserve the reasoning behind decisions—not only the final output.

In practice, that means turning ambiguous ideas and scattered evidence into specifications, decision maps, experiments, and artifacts that other people can inspect, test, and continue. I use AI throughout research and development, but judgment and direction remain human responsibilities.

My research draws mainly from cybernetics, domain modeling, and formal methods. Category theory and Lean give us ways to test specific ideas about refinement, translation, and information loss.

I am interested in building systems that expand what people can do without making their work harder to understand or govern.
